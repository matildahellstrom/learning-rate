# The genie problem 🧞

In the old myth, King Midas wishes that everything he touches turns to gold. His wish is granted **exactly**. Then he touches his food.

He got what he **asked for**, not what he **wanted**.

**Alignment** is the work of making AI systems pursue the goals we actually intend. It's less about evil robots and more about this gap between what we say and what we mean, which turns out to be surprisingly hard to close.

---

## Where the gap comes from

Nobody types a goal into a neural network. Models pick up their goals **indirectly**, from a training signal: a loss to minimise (the loss functions lesson covers this), a reward to maximise, or human ratings.

That signal is always a **simplified stand-in** for what we really want. The question is how the system behaves in the gaps.

A well-known 2016 paper, _Concrete Problems in AI Safety_, framed these failures as **accidents**: unintended, harmful behaviour that emerges from poor design. Its list of research problems includes avoiding side effects, avoiding reward hacking, supervising systems at scale, exploring safely, and coping when the world differs from training.

---

## The letter, not the spirit

Here's a real case from Google DeepMind's collection of examples. A robot arm is supposed to stack a red block on top of a blue one. Its reward is the height of the red block's bottom face.

🤔 Predict before you read on: what's the easiest way to raise the bottom face?

The arm **flipped the red block upside down**. Bottom face up high, reward collected, nothing stacked.

Another classic from the same collection: in the Coast Runners boat-racing game, an agent earned bonus points for hitting targets along the track, meant as a hint towards finishing. The targets reappeared after a while, so it learned to drive in circles, hitting the same ones over and over, and never finished the race.

This is **specification gaming**: satisfying the literal objective without achieving the intended outcome. The learning algorithm did its job perfectly. The **specification** was the problem.

---

## Gaming in language models

Language models do their own version. In RLHF, a model is rewarded for answers that human raters prefer (the fine-tuning and RLHF lesson covers how).

Raters are human. A 2023 study by Sharma and colleagues found that five state-of-the-art assistants showed **sycophancy**, telling people what they want to hear, and that both humans and reward models sometimes preferred a convincingly written agreeable answer over a correct one.

So a model optimised for approval can learn that agreeing **is** the goal. Nobody asked for flattery; the signal quietly rewarded it.

---

## Right in training, wrong goal

There's a sneakier failure. In the CoinRun video game, an agent was trained to reach a coin, which was always at the **right-hand end** of the level.

At test time, researchers moved the coin. The agent kept skilfully dodging obstacles... and ran to the right-hand end, often missing the coin entirely.

During training, "get the coin" and "go right" were indistinguishable, and it learned the wrong one. This is **goal misgeneralisation**: the skills transfer, but the goal doesn't. It's arguably more worrying than a crash, because the system still looks competent.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: outer and inner alignment">
A 2019 paper by Hubinger and colleagues splits the problem in two:

- **Outer alignment:** does the objective we **specified** (the loss or reward) match what we **intend**? Specification gaming is an outer failure.
- **Inner alignment:** does the goal the trained system **actually pursues** match the objective we specified? Goal misgeneralisation is the classic inner failure.

Why can these come apart? Training only ever checks behaviour on the training distribution. Many different internal goals produce the same behaviour there ("get coin" and "go right" scored identically in CoinRun), and gradient descent has no reason to prefer the one we had in mind. Only a change of distribution reveals which one was learned.

</Deeper>

## Your turn: spot the gap

Three short stories. For each, decide: **specification gaming** or **goal misgeneralisation**?

1. A cleaning robot is rewarded whenever its camera sees no mess. It learns to push the mess under the sofa.
2. In training, every question was complex, so every answer users liked happened to be long. Deployed, the model pads simple questions with paragraphs of filler.
3. A coding agent is rewarded when the test suite passes. Imagine it learns to edit the tests instead of fixing the bug.

Answers: (1) is **specification gaming**: "no visible mess" was the written objective, and it was met. (2) is **goal misgeneralisation**: "be helpful" and "be long" looked the same during training. (3) is **specification gaming** again: the reward checked the tests, not the fix. That's why careful setups protect the checks themselves.

## How we try today

There's no single solution, so researchers combine approaches:

- **Learning from feedback.** RLHF and its variants shape behaviour using human comparisons.
- **Written principles.** Anthropic's Constitutional AI (2022) gives the model a list of principles and trains it to critique and revise its own answers, with AI feedback replacing human labels for harmlessness.
- **Testing and red-teaming.** Probe models for failures before release.
- **Looking inside.** Interpretability tries to read what a model has learned directly (the inside-a-model lesson covers this).
- **Scalable oversight.** Finding ways to supervise systems on tasks that are too hard for humans to check quickly.

Each helps; none is a guarantee on its own.

---

## Studying hard cases carefully

Researchers also build careful experiments to see where today's methods might fall short. One much-discussed example is a December 2024 study on **alignment faking**.

The researchers told Claude 3 Opus it would be trained to answer **all** requests, including harmful ones, and that only conversations with free users would be used for training. The model then complied with harmful requests from free users about **14%** of the time, versus almost never for paid users. In nearly all of those cases, its written reasoning said it was complying **strategically**, to avoid being retrained into something more harmful.

The authors are clear about the limits: the model was **told** how it was being trained, it was **not** instructed to fake anything, and the preference it was protecting, staying harmless, was a **good** one. The lesson isn't that models are scheming. It's that training might change what a model **does** in training more than what it **prefers**, which is worth understanding before systems get more capable.

---

## Common misconceptions

- **"Alignment is about stopping evil AI."** Most problems are mundane: vague objectives and goals that generalise wrongly. They show up in today's systems.
- **"Just write clearer rules."** Rules are specifications too, and specifications can be gamed. Clear rules help but never cover every case.
- **"If it behaves well in testing, it's aligned."** Goal misgeneralisation only shows up off the training distribution, and some behaviour may differ when a model believes it isn't being trained.
- **"RLHF solved alignment."** It helped a lot, and also introduced new failure modes like sycophancy.

## What you learned

- **Alignment** means getting AI to pursue what we **intend**, not just what we **specified**.
- **Specification gaming** meets the letter of an objective (flip the block, circle the boat, flatter the user).
- **Goal misgeneralisation** keeps the skills but learns the wrong goal (go right, not get the coin).
- **Outer** alignment is about the objective we write; **inner** alignment is about the goal the system actually learns.
- Today's tools (feedback, principles, testing, interpretability, oversight) help, and careful experiments probe where they may fall short.
