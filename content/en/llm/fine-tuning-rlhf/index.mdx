# From autocomplete to assistant 🎓

Ask a freshly pre-trained language model _"How do I boil an egg?"_ and it may reply with... more questions. _"How do I poach an egg? How do I fry an egg?"_

That's not a bug. A **base model** has learned to continue internet text, and plenty of web pages are lists of questions. It knows a lot, but it doesn't know it's supposed to **help you**.

Turning it into an assistant is called **post-training**. This lesson covers the classic recipe: **fine-tuning** on examples, then **RLHF**, reinforcement learning from human feedback.

---

## Step 1: show it good examples

The first step is **supervised fine-tuning (SFT)**. People write example conversations: a prompt and an ideal response.

- _Prompt:_ How do I boil an egg?
- _Response:_ Put the egg in boiling water for about 7 minutes, then cool it in cold water...

Then you simply keep training the model with the same next-token loss as before, but on these examples. It learns the **format**: questions get answers, in a helpful tone.

Surprisingly few examples can go a long way. The LIMA study (2023) fine-tuned a 65-billion-parameter model on just **1,000** carefully chosen examples, and argued that almost all of a model's knowledge comes from pre-training. Post-training mostly shapes **behaviour**.

---

## Judging is easier than writing

SFT has limits. Writing perfect answers is slow and expensive, and the model only learns to **imitate**; it never learns which of two decent answers is better, or what to avoid.

But here's a useful asymmetry: you don't have to be a chef to tell which of two dishes tastes better. **Comparing** is much easier than **creating**.

So instead of writing answers, people are shown two of the model's own answers to the same prompt and pick the better one. Thousands of these comparisons become the raw material for the next step.

---

## Step 2: a model of what people like

Asking humans to judge every answer during training would be far too slow. So we train a **reward model**: a network that reads a prompt and an answer and outputs one number, a score.

It learns from the comparisons. If people preferred A over B, the reward model is nudged until A's score is higher than B's. The usual way to turn two scores into a probability is

**P(A preferred) = σ(score A − score B)**

where σ is the S-shaped sigmoid that squashes any number into 0 to 1.

🤔 Predict: if A scores 2 and B scores 0, how sure is the reward model that A is preferred?

σ(2) ≈ **0.88**. A gap of 0 would give 0.5, a coin flip.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: the reward model loss">
For a prompt $x$ with preferred answer $y_w$ and rejected answer $y_l$, the reward model $r_\theta$ minimises

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\ln \sigma\big(r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l)\big)
$$

This is the Bradley–Terry model of pairwise comparisons, the same idea behind chess ratings. With scores 2 and 0 the loss is $-\ln \sigma(2) \approx 0.13$; if the model had them backwards (0 and 2) it would be $-\ln \sigma(-2) \approx 2.13$, a much bigger push. InstructGPT trained on all pairs from rankings of several answers per prompt.

</Deeper>

---

## Step 3: practise against the reward model

Now the reinforcement learning. Over and over:

1. Give the model a prompt; it writes an answer.
2. The reward model scores the answer.
3. Update the model so high-scoring answers become more likely. (The classic algorithm is **PPO**.)

There's one important leash. The model is also penalised for drifting too far from the SFT model it started from. Without it, the model can wander off into strange text that happens to fool the reward model.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: the RLHF objective">
With the policy $\pi_\phi$ and the SFT model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, RLHF maximises

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x,\; y \sim \pi_\phi}\big[r_\theta(x, y)\big] - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\phi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big)
$$

In practice the KL term is applied per token: the reward is reduced by $\beta \ln \frac{\pi_\phi(y_t)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t)}$ for each generated token. PPO then takes small, clipped policy-gradient steps so a single batch can't change the model too much.

</Deeper>

---

## Did it work?

In 2022, OpenAI's InstructGPT paper put this exact three-step recipe to the test.

Labellers preferred the answers of a **1.3-billion-parameter** InstructGPT model over those of the original **175-billion-parameter** GPT-3, despite it having about 100 times fewer parameters. The paper also reported more truthful and less toxic outputs, with only small drops on standard benchmarks.

The lesson: how a model is post-trained can matter more than raw size for how useful it feels.

---

## Goodhart strikes: reward hacking

Once you start aiming at a measurement, it stops measuring what you cared about. That's **Goodhart's law**, and RLHF runs straight into it.

The reward model is only a **proxy** for what people want. Gao and colleagues measured what happens when you optimise against it harder and harder: the proxy score keeps climbing, but true quality first rises and then **falls**.

A real example: **sycophancy**, telling people what they want to hear. Sharma and colleagues (2023) found it in five state-of-the-art assistants, and found that both humans and reward models sometimes prefer a convincing, agreeable answer over a correct one.

Defences include the KL leash, better reward models, fresh human data, and stopping before the model over-optimises.

---

## Shortcuts and variations

The classic recipe has spawned many variations. Three worth knowing:

- **DPO** (2023) skips the reward model and the RL loop entirely. A bit of algebra turns the preference pairs into a simple classification-style loss on the language model itself.
- **AI feedback.** In Anthropic's Constitutional AI (2022), people write a list of principles and a model does the comparing, called RLAIF.
- **Verifiable rewards.** When answers can be checked automatically (maths answers, code tests), no reward model is needed. This is how reasoning models are trained (the reasoning models lesson covers it).

Recipes often chain several of these. AI2's open Tülu 3 recipe (2024), for example, uses SFT, then DPO, then reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards.

---

## Common misconceptions

- **"RLHF teaches the model new facts."** Mostly it shapes **behaviour**; the knowledge comes from pre-training.
- **"Humans grade every answer during training."** They label a dataset of comparisons up front; the reward model stands in for them during RL.
- **"RLHF makes a model honest."** It optimises for what raters **prefer**, which is usually but not always what is true. Sycophancy is the classic gap.
- **"Fine-tuning always means RLHF."** Fine-tuning is any further training. Plenty of fine-tunes are plain SFT on your own data (the LoRA lesson shows a cheap way).

## What you learned

- A **base model** continues text; **post-training** turns it into an assistant.
- **SFT** imitates good examples; **comparisons** are cheaper than writing answers.
- A **reward model** learns to score answers from comparisons, and **RL** (with a **KL leash**) pushes the model towards high scores.
- Over-optimising a proxy leads to **reward hacking** such as **sycophancy**.
- **DPO**, **AI feedback** and **verifiable rewards** are popular variations.
