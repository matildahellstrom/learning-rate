# The chef and the apprentice 🧑‍🍳

A world-class chef can't cook in every restaurant. So she trains an apprentice.

She could hand over a recipe book: dish in, dish out. But a good apprentice learns more by watching her taste: _"Needs salt. Maybe lemon? Definitely not sugar."_ Her **hunches about the wrong options** say a lot about how she thinks.

**Distillation** does the same with neural networks. A big, expensive **teacher** model trains a small, cheap **student** to imitate it, hunches and all.

---

## Why shrink a model at all?

Big models are slow and costly to run. Every answer burns compute, and they won't fit on a phone.

You could train a small model from scratch on the same data. But a small model trained to **imitate a big one** often ends up noticeably better than the same small model trained alone.

The idea goes back to work by Caruana and colleagues on "model compression" in 2006. Hinton, Vinyals and Dean made it famous in 2015 by squeezing a whole ensemble of models into one network, and named it **distillation**.

---

## Hard labels vs soft labels

Ordinary training uses **hard labels**: this picture is a "2", full stop. Every wrong answer counts as equally wrong.

A trained teacher gives **soft labels**, a probability for every option. Hinton's paper uses handwritten digits: one particular 2 might get a one-in-a-million chance of being a 3, and a one-in-a-billion chance of being a 7. Those tiny numbers still say _this 2 looks a bit like a 3_.

Language models work the same way. After _"The cat sat on the"_, a teacher might say:

- **mat** 0.84 · **sofa** 0.11 · **floor** 0.04 · **moon** 0.002

The hard label is just "mat". The soft label also teaches that sofa is fine and moon is silly. Hinton called this extra information **dark knowledge**.

---

## Turn up the temperature 🌡️

There's a snag: the interesting probabilities are **tiny**. They barely move the loss, so the student hardly notices them.

The fix is the same knob you meet in sampling: **temperature**. Divide the teacher's raw scores (logits) by a temperature $T$ before the softmax. (The softmax lesson covers this.)

The logits behind the example above are 5, 3, 2 and −1. Watch what $T$ does:

- **T = 1:** mat 0.84 · sofa 0.11 · floor 0.04 · moon 0.002
- **T = 2:** mat 0.61 · sofa 0.22 · floor 0.14 · moon 0.03
- **T = 4:** mat 0.43 · sofa 0.26 · floor 0.21 · moon 0.10

🤔 Notice what stays the same and what changes before you read on.

The **ranking** never changes; mat always wins. But the gaps shrink, so the hunches about sofa and floor become loud enough for the student to learn from. During distillation, teacher and student both use the high temperature. Afterwards, the student runs at $T = 1$ as usual.

---

## The training recipe

The student sees the same inputs as the teacher and is trained on a mix of two goals:

1. **Match the teacher:** make your softened probabilities close to the teacher's softened probabilities.
2. **Get the right answer:** the usual loss against the hard label, at $T = 1$ (when labels exist).

Usually the first part carries most of the weight. Notice the teacher only has to **run**, never train, so you can generate its soft labels once and reuse them.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: the loss and the T² factor">
Write $z$ for the student's logits, $v$ for the teacher's, and $\sigma_T(z)_i = \frac{e^{z_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j/T}}$ for the softmax at temperature $T$. A common form of the loss is

$$
\mathcal{L} = \lambda\, T^2 \, \mathrm{KL}\big(\sigma_T(v) \,\|\, \sigma_T(z)\big) + (1 - \lambda)\, \mathrm{CE}\big(y, \sigma_1(z)\big)
$$

The gradient of the soft cross-entropy with respect to a student logit is

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial z_i}\,\mathrm{CE}\big(\sigma_T(v), \sigma_T(z)\big) = \frac{1}{T}\big(\sigma_T(z)_i - \sigma_T(v)_i\big)
$$

and the difference in brackets itself shrinks roughly like $1/T$ when $T$ is large compared with the logits. So the soft-target gradients scale like $1/T^2$, and Hinton et al. multiply by $T^2$ to keep the balance between the two terms when $T$ changes.

In the high-temperature limit (with zero-mean logits) minimising the soft loss becomes matching the logits themselves: $\tfrac12 \sum_i (z_i - v_i)^2$, an older compression trick.

</Deeper>

## Worked example: two students

The teacher's soft label for the next word is **mat 0.6 · sofa 0.3 · floor 0.1**. The true next word was "mat". Two students answer:

- **Student A:** mat 0.6 · sofa 0.3 · floor 0.1 (a perfect copy)
- **Student B:** mat 0.9 · sofa 0.05 · floor 0.05 (overconfident)

🤔 Which student does each kind of training prefer?

**Hard label** (cross-entropy, $-\ln$ of the probability on "mat"): A scores $-\ln 0.6 \approx 0.51$, B scores $-\ln 0.9 \approx 0.11$. Hard labels reward B for being sure.

**Soft label** (cross-entropy against the teacher, $-\sum p \ln q$): A scores about **0.90**, B about **1.26**. Distillation prefers A, the student who learned that sofa is a reasonable answer too.

You can check A's number: $-(0.6 \ln 0.6 + 0.3 \ln 0.3 + 0.1 \ln 0.1) \approx 0.31 + 0.36 + 0.23 = 0.90$.

## Two flavours in the wild

**Match the probabilities.** The student copies the teacher's full distribution, as above. You need access to the teacher's logits. DistilBERT (2019) did this and came out 40% smaller and 60% faster than BERT, keeping 97% of its language-understanding performance. Google trained the 2B and 9B Gemma 2 models (2024) with distillation instead of plain next-token prediction.

**Learn from the outputs.** Let the teacher write answers, then fine-tune the student on that text. Kim and Rush called this **sequence-level** distillation in 2016. DeepSeek used it in 2025 to pass the reasoning style of its big R1 model to six smaller models (1.5B to 70B parameters, built on Qwen and Llama).

Distilled students also make great drafters for speculative decoding, since a drafter is only useful when it agrees with the big model.

---

## Common misconceptions

- **"The student becomes as good as the teacher."** Usually it gets close, not equal. A smaller network has less room to store knowledge, so some gap usually remains.
- **"Distillation copies weights."** The core idea is learning from the teacher's **outputs**, so the student can have a completely different shape. (Some recipes give the student a head start: DistilBERT began with every other layer of BERT.)
- **"It's the same as quantization."** Quantization keeps the same model and stores its numbers more coarsely. Distillation trains a **new**, smaller model.
- **"You need the original training data."** Any inputs that make the teacher show its knowledge can work, including text the teacher wrote itself.

## What you learned

- **Distillation** trains a small **student** to imitate a big **teacher**.
- **Soft labels** carry dark knowledge: which wrong answers are plausible.
- A higher **temperature** flattens the teacher's probabilities so those hunches are loud enough to learn from; a $T^2$ factor keeps the loss balanced.
- You can match the teacher's **probabilities**, or simply train on **text the teacher wrote**.
