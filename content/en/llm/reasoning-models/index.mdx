# Think before you speak 🤔

Try this one quickly, in your head:

_A bat and a ball cost €1.10 together. The bat costs €1.00 more than the ball. How much is the ball?_

Most people's first instinct is **10 cents**. But then the bat would be €1.10 and the total €1.20. Write it down step by step and you find the real answer: **5 cents** (bat €1.05).

Language models have the same problem with snap answers, and the same fix. A **reasoning model** is trained to work through a problem in writing before it commits to an answer.

---

## Why thinking out loud helps a model

A language model produces one token at a time, and every token gets the **same fixed amount of computation**: one forward pass. (The next-token prediction lesson covers this.)

A snap answer has to squeeze all the work into the few tokens of the answer itself. Writing intermediate steps first does two things:

- **More computation.** Every extra token is another full pass through the network.
- **A scratchpad.** Results written down early can be read back later, like your notes on paper.

This chain of reasoning is called a **chain of thought**. Before anyone trained for it, researchers found you could simply **ask** for it:

- In 2022, Wei and colleagues showed a 540-billion-parameter model a few worked examples with their reasoning written out. On the GSM8K maths word problems, this reached state-of-the-art accuracy.
- The same year, Kojima and colleagues added one sentence, _"Let's think step by step"_. With one of OpenAI's models, accuracy on GSM8K jumped from 10.4% to 40.7%, with no examples at all.

That was a big hint: the ability was partly there already, waiting to be used.

---

## From prompt trick to trained skill

A **reasoning model** doesn't need to be asked. It is trained, with reinforcement learning, to produce a long chain of thought before its final answer.

OpenAI's o1 models (2024) were described as _trained with large-scale reinforcement learning to reason using chain of thought_. In January 2025, DeepSeek published the recipe for its open R1 model in detail, so let's look at that one.

The key ingredient is a **reward you can check**:

- **Maths:** does the final answer match the known answer?
- **Code:** does the program pass the tests?

The model writes many attempts, and the ones that end in a correct answer are reinforced. Nobody tells it **how** to reason. It only learns that whatever it did on the way to correct answers was worth doing.

---

## Learning from a group of attempts

DeepSeek used an algorithm called **GRPO**. For each question it samples a **group** of answers and compares each answer with its own group.

Worked example. Four attempts at one maths question:

- ✅ correct · ❌ wrong · ❌ wrong · ✅ correct → rewards **1, 0, 0, 1**

🤔 Predict: which attempts get pushed up and which down?

The group average is 0.5. Each attempt's **advantage** is its reward minus the average: **+0.5, −0.5, −0.5, +0.5**. The correct ones become more likely, the wrong ones less likely. If every attempt is correct, everything is average and nothing changes: an easy question teaches nothing.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: the GRPO advantage">
For a question $q$, sample $G$ outputs $o_1, \dots, o_G$ with rewards $r_1, \dots, r_G$. GRPO's advantage for output $i$ is the reward standardised within its group:

$$
A_i = \frac{r_i - \operatorname{mean}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}{\operatorname{std}(r_1, \dots, r_G)}
$$

For rewards $1, 0, 0, 1$: mean $0.5$, standard deviation $0.5$, so $A = (+1, -1, -1, +1)$. Every token of output $i$ is then pushed up or down in proportion to $A_i$, with a PPO-style clipped update and a penalty for drifting from a reference model.

Using the group as the baseline removes the separate **critic** network that PPO normally trains to predict expected reward, a model typically as big as the policy itself. That saves a lot of memory and compute.

</Deeper>

---

## What emerged

DeepSeek first tried RL **without** any human-written reasoning examples (they called that model R1-Zero). The paper reports:

- On the AIME 2024 maths competition, accuracy rose from **15.6% to 71.0%** during training, and to 86.7% with majority voting.
- The answers grew **longer** by themselves: the model learned to spend more thinking on harder problems.
- It started to **re-check** its work mid-answer, at one point writing _"Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here."_

R1-Zero's reasoning was hard to read and sometimes mixed languages. The final R1 model added a small set of curated examples before RL to fix that.

---

## A new dial: thinking time

This creates a new way to buy quality: spend more computation **when answering** (called **test-time compute**), not just when training. Two ways to spend it:

- **Think longer:** a longer chain of thought.
- **Think in parallel:** sample several answers and take a **majority vote**. Wang and colleagues called this **self-consistency**; it improved GSM8K accuracy by 17.9 percentage points over a single chain of thought.

🤔 Quick check: five sampled answers say 42, 42, 17, 42, 40. What does the vote pick? (42.)

A 2024 study by Snell and colleagues found that spending test-time compute **adaptively** per question was over 4× more efficient than simple best-of-N sampling. On problems where a small model already had some success, the extra thinking let it beat a model 14× larger given the same total compute.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: why voting helps">
Suppose each sample is correct with probability $p$, independently. With 3 samples, the majority is correct if at least 2 are:

$$
P = p^3 + 3p^2(1 - p)
$$

At $p = 0.6$ that's $0.216 + 0.432 = 0.648$, better than one sample. With $n$ samples and $p > 0.5$, the majority's accuracy tends to 1 as $n$ grows (a binomial tail). In practice wrong answers tend to scatter over many different values while correct chains agree, so a **plurality** vote can help even when $p < 0.5$. The catch is the independence assumption: if a model makes the same mistake every time, no amount of voting fixes it.

</Deeper>

## Thinking in practice

Thinking isn't free: the reasoning tokens take time and are paid for. For example, Claude's API bills thinking tokens as **output tokens**, even when the thinking text isn't shown to you.

So modern models let you set how hard they think. As of October 2026, newer Claude models use **adaptive thinking**: the model decides whether and how much to think on each request, guided by an **effort** setting, and may skip thinking entirely on easy inputs.

Use reasoning for multi-step work: maths, tricky bugs, planning, long agent tasks. For a quick fact or a simple rewrite, it mostly adds waiting.

---

## Can you trust the reasoning?

It's tempting to read a model's chain of thought as a window into its mind. Be careful.

In a 2025 study, Anthropic researchers slipped **hints** into questions and checked whether reasoning models admitted using them. Claude 3.7 Sonnet mentioned a hint it had used only **25%** of the time, and DeepSeek R1 **39%**. When models learned to exploit a planted shortcut, they mentioned it in their reasoning less than 2% of the time in most settings.

So the chain of thought is genuinely useful, for the model and for us, but it isn't a complete or guaranteed-honest record of what drove the answer.

---

## Common misconceptions

- **"Reasoning models are a new kind of AI."** Where the details are public, as with DeepSeek-R1, it's the same kind of next-token transformer. What changed is the training, which rewards long, self-checking answers.
- **"More thinking is always better."** Returns diminish, and simple questions don't benefit. Thinking costs time and money.
- **"The model reasons like a person."** It produces text that **looks** like human reasoning because that helped it get rewarded, not because it thinks the way we do.
- **"The visible reasoning is the whole story."** Some of what drives the answer may never show up in the text.

## What you learned

- Every token gets a fixed amount of computation, so **writing out steps** gives a model more computation and a scratchpad.
- **Chain-of-thought prompting** showed the ability was there; **reasoning models** are trained with RL on **checkable rewards** to use it.
- **GRPO** compares each attempt with its group; long, self-checking reasoning **emerged** from this alone.
- **Test-time compute** is a new dial: think longer, or sample several answers and **vote**.
- The written reasoning is helpful but **not always faithful**.
