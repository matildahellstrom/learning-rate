# Sticky notes on a giant textbook 📒

Imagine you're a teacher with a brilliant 1,000-page textbook. You want it to fit **your** class: a few corrections, local examples, a different emphasis.

You could reprint the whole book with your edits. Expensive! Or you could leave the book alone and add a stack of **sticky notes**.

**LoRA** (Low-Rank Adaptation) is the sticky-note approach to fine-tuning. The big model stays frozen; you train a tiny add-on that nudges its behaviour.

---

## Why full fine-tuning hurts

Fine-tuning means continuing to train a pre-trained model on your own examples. (The fine-tuning lesson covers why.) Done the straightforward way, **every** weight gets updated.

That's painful for big models:

- **Memory.** Besides the weights, training stores a gradient and optimiser state for every one of them (the popular Adam optimiser keeps two running averages per weight). With Adam and mixed precision (a 16-bit working copy plus a 32-bit master copy of the weights), that's about 16 bytes per parameter: around **112 GB** for a 7-billion-parameter model, before counting activations.
- **Storage.** Every fine-tuned version is a full copy of the model. Ten customers, ten giant files.

So people asked: do we really need to change all those numbers?

---

## The low-rank bet

The LoRA authors bet that the **change** a model needs for a new task is simple, even when the model itself is huge.

Think of a giant spreadsheet: songs as columns, people as rows, each cell a rating. It looks like millions of independent numbers, but much of it is explained by a few hidden "taste" factors, like how much each person likes guitars and how guitar-heavy each song is.

A matrix that can be built from a few such factors is called **low-rank**. (The SVD lesson covers this properly.) LoRA assumes the fine-tuning **update** to each adapted weight matrix is low-rank, even if the weights themselves aren't.

---

## How LoRA works

Take one weight matrix $W$ in the model, size $d \times k$. Normally a layer computes $W x$.

LoRA freezes $W$ and adds a detour through two skinny matrices:

- $A$, size $r \times k$, squeezes the input down to just $r$ numbers,
- $B$, size $d \times r$, spreads them back out.

The layer now computes $W x + B A x$. The **rank** $r$ is tiny (the LoRA paper mostly used 4 or 8), while $d$ and $k$ can be in the thousands.

Only $A$ and $B$ are trained. And $B$ starts at **zero**, so at the start $BA = 0$ and the model behaves exactly like the original. Training then grows the sticky notes from nothing.

---

## Count the savings

A typical matrix in a large model might be $4096 \times 4096$.

- **Full fine-tuning** trains all of it: 4096 × 4096 = **16,777,216** numbers.
- **LoRA with r = 8** trains $B$ (4096 × 8) and $A$ (8 × 4096): 32,768 + 32,768 = **65,536** numbers.

That's **256 times fewer**, about 0.4% of the original. 🤔 Predict before you read on: what does doubling the rank to 16 do? (It doubles the count to 131,072: still 128 times fewer.)

At scale the numbers get wild. For GPT-3 (175 billion parameters), the LoRA paper reports about **10,000× fewer** trainable parameters, training memory down from 1.2 TB to 350 GB, and saved checkpoints shrinking from roughly 350 GB to about 35 MB.

---

## A rank-1 sticky note, by hand

Here's the smallest possible version. Let $B$ be a column and $A$ a row:

$$
B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Multiply them (each entry is a row number of $B$ times a column number of $A$):

$$
BA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Check one entry yourself: row 2, column 3 is $2 \times (-1) = -2$.

Six numbers describe a $3 \times 3$ update of nine. Every row is the same pattern (1, 0, −1) scaled by 1, 2 or 0. That's what **rank 1** means. A rank-$r$ update is a sum of $r$ such patterns.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: rank, SVD and the scaling factor">
Any matrix $M$ has a singular value decomposition $M = \sum_i \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top$ with $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0$. Keeping the top $r$ terms gives the **best** rank-$r$ approximation in the least-squares sense (the Eckart–Young theorem). LoRA's bet is that the ideal update $\Delta W$ has most of its energy in a few singular directions, so $\Delta W \approx BA$ loses little. The LoRA paper found that a rank as small as 1 was enough on some GPT-3 tasks, though not on every task.

In practice the update is scaled:

$$
h = W x + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A x
$$

so that changing $r$ doesn't require re-tuning the learning rate. A is initialised randomly and $B = 0$. Notice the asymmetry in the first step: $\partial L / \partial A = \tfrac{\alpha}{r} B^\top (\partial L / \partial h)\, x^\top = 0$, while $\partial L / \partial B = \tfrac{\alpha}{r}(\partial L / \partial h)(A x)^\top$ is not, so $B$ moves first and $A$ follows.

Parameter count per matrix: $r(d + k)$ instead of $dk$. The memory saving is smaller than this ratio suggests, because the frozen weights and the activations still have to sit in memory.

</Deeper>

---

## Swap, merge, squeeze

Two practical superpowers:

- **No slowdown when serving.** After training you can **merge**: $W' = W + BA$ is one matrix of the original shape, so the model runs exactly as fast as before.
- **Swappable skills.** Or keep adapters separate: one shared base model plus a small file per task or customer, swapped in as needed.

And one combo: **QLoRA** stores the frozen base model in 4-bit precision (the quantisation lesson covers this) and trains LoRA adapters on top. In 2023 that made it possible to fine-tune a 65-billion-parameter model on a **single 48 GB GPU**.

---

## Common misconceptions

- **"LoRA always matches full fine-tuning."** Not always. A 2024 study on code and maths found standard LoRA learned less of the new domain than full fine-tuning, but **forgot less** of what the model already knew. Whether that trade is worth it depends on your task.
- **"LoRA makes the model smaller or faster."** It makes **training** and **storing fine-tunes** cheaper. The model itself is as big as before.
- **"Bigger rank is always better."** Higher ranks cost more and often help little. Start small and check.
- **"LoRA is only for language models."** It works on any big weight matrix; it's popular for image generators too.

## What you learned

- **Full fine-tuning** updates every weight and needs a lot of memory and storage.
- **LoRA** freezes the model and learns a low-rank update $BA$ with only $r(d + k)$ numbers per matrix.
- $B$ starts at zero, so training begins from the original model, and merging afterwards costs **no extra inference time**.
- LoRA trades a little learning power for big savings, and **forgets less** of what the model knew.
