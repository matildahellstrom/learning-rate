# What does a computer see? 👁️

You look at a photo and see a cat on a sofa, instantly.

A computer sees something more like this, for a small patch near the cat's ear:

```
112 118 121 130
109 115 160 201
104 158 210 214
101 150 207 219
```

Just numbers. No "cat", no "ear", not even "edge". Every vision model, from a phone's face unlock to an image generator, starts from a grid like this.

This lesson shows how a picture becomes numbers, how those numbers are arranged into a **tensor**, and why that arrangement matters for everything that comes later.

---

## Pixels are numbers

A digital image is a grid of tiny squares called **pixels**. In a **greyscale** image each pixel is one number saying how bright it is.

The usual scale runs from **0 (black)** to **255 (white)**, with greys in between. Why 255? It's the largest number that fits in **8 bits** (one byte), which the Pillow image library calls mode `L`: "8-bit pixels, grayscale".

So the grid in the hook is a patch that's darker at the top left and brighter at the bottom right: perhaps where a dark background meets a pale ear.

A greyscale image that's 28 pixels wide and 28 tall is simply a 28 × 28 grid: a **matrix** of 784 numbers, just like the ones in the vectors and matrices lesson.

---

## Colour: three numbers per pixel

Screens mix colours from red, green and blue light. So a colour pixel stores three numbers, one for each, each from 0 to 255. Pillow calls this mode `RGB`: "3x8-bit pixels, true color".

- (255, 0, 0) is pure red.
- (0, 0, 0) is black; (255, 255, 255) is white.
- (128, 128, 128) is a mid grey: equal amounts of all three.

🤔 Predict: what colour is (255, 255, 0)?

Red and green fully on, blue off. Mixing red and green **light** gives **yellow**. (Paint mixes differently, which is why this one surprises people.)

Each of these three layers (all the red values, all the green values, all the blue values) is called a **channel**. A colour image is three greyscale-like grids stacked on top of each other.

---

## From grid to tensor

Stack three grids and you get a block of numbers with three directions: height, width and channels. A block of numbers with any number of directions is called a **tensor**.

- A single number is a 0-D tensor (a scalar).
- A list is 1-D (a vector).
- A grid is 2-D (a matrix).
- A colour image is **3-D**.

Each direction is an **axis** (or dimension), and the list of their sizes is the tensor's **shape**. Stanford's CS231n course uses a cat photo that's 248 pixels wide and 400 tall: its shape is 400 × 248 × 3, which is **297,600 numbers**.

🤔 Predict: many vision models take 224 × 224 colour images. How many numbers is that?

224 × 224 = 50,176 pixels, times 3 channels = **150,528 numbers**. A small picture by phone standards, but already a lot of inputs.

---

## Same numbers, different order

Here's a detail that trips up almost everyone at least once. The three axes can be stored in different orders.

- **Channels last (H, W, C):** height, width, then the three colour values. This is how image files load in Pillow and NumPy, and it's the default in Keras.
- **Channels first (C, H, W):** all the red values, then all the green, then all the blue. This is what PyTorch models expect.

PyTorch's `ToTensor` converts between them: its docs say it turns an image of shape "(H x W x C) in the range [0, 255]" into a tensor "of shape (C x H x W) in the range [0.0, 1.0]".

Neither order is better. They're the same numbers, laid out differently in memory. Mix them up and you'll often get a shape error, which is the lucky case. The unlucky case is forcing the numbers into the right shape without actually reordering the axes: the shape looks correct, there's no error, and the pixels are scrambled.

---

## Batches: a fourth axis

Models rarely see one image at a time. Training takes each gradient step on a small random **batch** of examples (the gradient descent lesson mentions this), and the images in a batch are processed together.

So images are stacked along one more axis, the batch axis, which comes first. A batch of 32 colour images of 224 × 224 in PyTorch has shape

**(32, 3, 224, 224)**, often written **(N, C, H, W)**.

That's 32 × 150,528 = **4,816,896 numbers**, all fed through the model together. Keras's channels-last layout is (N, H, W, C) instead.

Video adds yet another axis, for time. The idea scales: each new kind of "direction" in the data becomes one more axis.

---

## Scaling the numbers

Models learn best when their inputs are small numbers centred near zero, rather than integers up to 255. So images go through two common steps.

1. **Rescale** to 0 to 1 by dividing by 255. A pixel of 200 becomes 200 ÷ 255 ≈ 0.784.
2. **Normalise** each channel: subtract an average and divide by a typical spread (the standard deviation).

For PyTorch's ImageNet-trained ResNet-50, the docs list the averages as 0.485, 0.456 and 0.406 (red, green, blue) and the spreads as 0.229, 0.224 and 0.225. So a red value of 0.784 becomes (0.784 − 0.485) ÷ 0.229 ≈ **1.31**.

A pre-trained model expects exactly the preprocessing it was trained with. Skip it, and the model sees inputs that look nothing like its training data.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: memory and number types">
Stored as 8-bit integers (`uint8`), each number takes 1 byte, so one 224 × 224 RGB image is 150,528 bytes, about 150 kB.

After conversion to 32-bit floats (`float32`), each number takes 4 bytes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
150{,}528 \times 4 &= 602{,}112 \text{ bytes} \\
&\approx 602 \text{ kB}
\end{aligned}
$$

The batch of 32 from earlier is then about 19 MB, before the model has computed anything. This is one reason training images are often kept as compressed files or `uint8` until the last moment.

Normalisation per channel $c$ is

$$
x' = \frac{x / 255 - \mu_c}{\sigma_c}
$$

with $\mu_c$ and $\sigma_c$ measured on the training set.

</Deeper>

## Why not just flatten it?

You could unroll the whole tensor into one long list and feed it to an ordinary neural network, where every input connects to every neuron.

CS231n does the maths. A 32 × 32 × 3 image gives each neuron 3,072 weights. That "still seems manageable", but a 200 × 200 × 3 image needs **120,000 weights per neuron**, and you want many neurons.

There's a deeper problem.

🤔 Predict: you shift a photo one pixel to the right. What happens to the flattened list?

Almost every number moves to a new position. To the network, it's a very different input, even though you'd call it the same picture. CS231n shows the same issue the other way round: images that are close in raw pixel distance are often nothing alike to a person.

Flattening throws away the one thing images give you for free: which pixels are **neighbours**. The Deep Learning book describes images as data with "a known grid-like topology". Models that respect that grid are the subject of the next lessons.

---

## Common misconceptions

- **"The computer sees the picture."** It gets a tensor of brightness values. Everything else (edges, ears, cats) has to be computed from those numbers.
- **"RGB means three separate images."** It's one image with three channels. Each pixel's three values belong together.
- **"Channels-first and channels-last are different data."** Same numbers, different order. The danger is mixing them up silently.
- **"Normalising changes what's in the picture."** It shifts and scales every value in a channel the same way, so within a channel brighter stays brighter. It changes the scale, not the content.
- **"Bigger tensors just need a bigger plain network."** Weight counts grow with every pixel, and a plain network still treats a shifted image as brand new. That's why vision uses special architectures.

## What you learned

- An image is a grid of **pixels**; each is a number from **0 to 255** in 8-bit images.
- Colour images have three **channels** (red, green, blue), making a **3-D tensor**.
- **Shape** lists the axis sizes: (H, W, C) channels-last or (C, H, W) channels-first.
- A **batch** adds a fourth axis: (N, C, H, W) in PyTorch.
- Inputs are **rescaled** and **normalised** to match how a model was trained.
- **Flattening** loses which pixels are neighbours, which motivates models built for grids.
