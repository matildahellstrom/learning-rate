# Librarian or farmer? 📚

Here's a puzzle from a study by the psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, as retold by 3Blue1Brown.

Steve is described as very shy and withdrawn, helpful but with little interest in people: "a meek and tidy soul", with "a need for order and structure, and a passion for detail".

Is Steve more likely to be a **librarian** or a **farmer**?

Most people say librarian. He sounds like one! But Kahneman and Tversky's point was that almost nobody stops to ask **how many** librarians and farmers there are. In their paper, they said that in the US there are about **20 farmers for every librarian**.

This lesson covers what a probability is, **conditional probability**, and **Bayes' rule**: the recipe for combining what you knew before with what you just learned. It ends with a spam filter built on it.

---

## A number for uncertainty

A **probability** is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). A fair die lands on 6 with probability 1/6, and on an even number with probability 3/6 = 1/2.

The Deep Learning book describes two ways to read that number:

- **Frequentist:** how often something happens if you repeat it many times, like dice rolls.
- **Bayesian:** a **degree of belief** about something that only happens once, like whether a particular patient has flu.

The book's point is that both obey **exactly the same rules**, so you don't need separate maths for each.

Two rules you'll use constantly:

- The probabilities of all the possible outcomes add up to **1**. (Softmax produces exactly this kind of list.)
- P(not A) = 1 − P(A).

---

## Zooming in: conditional probability

Often you want a probability **given** that something else is true. That's a **conditional probability**, written P(A | B): "the probability of A, given B".

The idea: **zoom in** on the cases where B happened, and ask what fraction of them also have A.

🤔 Predict: you draw a card from a shuffled deck and are told it's a face card (jack, queen or king). What's the probability it's a king?

There are 12 face cards and 4 of them are kings: **4/12 = 1/3**. Knowing it's a face card raised the chance from 4/52 to 1/3.

Watch out: **P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different numbers.** Almost every Dalmatian has spots, but only a small fraction of spotted animals are Dalmatians.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: the definition">
The Deep Learning book defines it as

$$
P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}
$$

which only makes sense when $P(B) > 0$. The book also warns that conditioning is not the same as acting: the probability that someone is from Germany given that they speak German is high, but teaching a random person German doesn't change where they're from.

</Deeper>

## Steve, by counting

Let's settle Steve with a representative crowd. 3Blue1Brown suggests picturing **200 farmers and 10 librarians**: the 20-to-1 ratio.

Now suppose your gut says **40%** of librarians fit Steve's description, but only **10%** of farmers do. (These are 3Blue1Brown's illustrative guesses, not measured numbers.)

🤔 Predict: in that crowd, how many librarians fit the description, and how many farmers?

- Librarians: 40% of 10 = **4**
- Farmers: 10% of 200 = **20**

Of the 24 people who fit, only 4 are librarians: **4/24 ≈ 16.7%**. Even though the description fits a librarian 4 times better, farmers still win, because there are so many more of them.

3Blue1Brown's mantra: new evidence should not completely determine your beliefs; it should **update** what you believed before.

---

## Bayes' rule

The counting has a formula. You have a **hypothesis** H (Steve is a librarian) and **evidence** E (the description).

**P(H | E) = P(H) × P(E | H) ÷ P(E)**

Each piece has a name:

- **Prior**, P(H): your belief before the evidence. Here 1/21.
- **Likelihood**, P(E | H): how well the evidence fits if H is true. Here 0.4.
- **Posterior**, P(H | E): your belief after the evidence. The answer.

P(E) is the total chance of seeing the evidence, from both sides: (1/21) × 0.4 + (20/21) × 0.1. Plug everything in and you get 0.4 ÷ (0.4 + 2) = **1/6 ≈ 16.7%**, the same as counting.

The Deep Learning book notes the rule is named after the Reverend Thomas Bayes, who first discovered a special case of it; the general version was discovered independently by Pierre-Simon Laplace.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: where it comes from">
Write $P(H \text{ and } E)$ two ways using the definition of conditional probability:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(H \text{ and } E) &= P(H \mid E)\,P(E) \\
&= P(E \mid H)\,P(H)
\end{aligned}
$$

Divide both by $P(E)$ and you have Bayes' rule. The denominator splits into the two cases:

$$
\begin{aligned}
P(E) &= P(E \mid H)\,P(H) \\
&\quad + P(E \mid \neg H)\,P(\neg H)
\end{aligned}
$$

</Deeper>

## The base-rate trap

Here's a **made-up** medical example that shows why the prior matters so much.

A condition affects **1 in 100** people. A test catches **90%** of people who have it, but also wrongly flags **10%** of people who don't. You test positive.

🤔 Predict: roughly how likely is it that you have the condition: about 90%, about 50%, or under 10%?

Count 1,000 people:

- 10 have it; 90% of them, **9**, test positive.
- 990 don't; 10% of them, **99**, test positive anyway.

Only 9 of the 108 positives are real: **9/108 ≈ 8.3%**. Under 10%!

The test is useful (it raised your chance from 1% to about 8%), but the condition is rare, so false alarms from the large healthy group outnumber the true cases. Ignoring the prior like this is called **base-rate neglect**.

---

## Updating again and again

Today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior. Say you take a **second**, independent test (in this made-up example, with the same error rates) and it's positive too.

The quickest way is with **odds**. Before the second test, the odds were 9 real to 99 false, or **1 to 11**. A positive result is 0.9 ÷ 0.1 = **9 times** as likely if you have the condition. Multiply: 1 to 11 becomes **9 to 11**, which is 9/20 = **45%**.

Two positives took you from 1% to 45%. Each piece of evidence **nudges** the belief; none of them replaces it.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: the odds form">
Dividing Bayes' rule for $H$ by Bayes' rule for $\neg H$ cancels $P(E)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{P(H \mid E)}{P(\neg H \mid E)}
&= \frac{P(H)}{P(\neg H)} \\
&\quad \times \frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid \neg H)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Posterior odds = prior odds × **likelihood ratio**. The first test: 1 to 99, times 9, is 9 to 99. This only works test after test if the tests are independent given whether you have the condition; two tests that make the same mistakes don't count twice.

</Deeper>

## A spam filter from Bayes

Treat each word of an email as a piece of evidence. "Viagra" is much more likely in spam; "meeting" much more likely in real mail. Combine them with Bayes' rule and you have a **naive Bayes** classifier.

The scikit-learn docs explain the "naive" part: it assumes the words (the **features**) are **independent of each other once you know the class**. That's clearly false ("free" and "offer" travel together), but it lets you multiply one simple probability per word. Despite the over-simplified assumption, the docs say these classifiers have worked well in practice, "famously document classification and spam filtering".

In 2002, Paul Graham's essay "A Plan for Spam" described a filter using a Bayesian combination of the spam probabilities of the fifteen most telling words (tokens) in each email. He reported that it missed fewer than 5 in 1,000 spams, with **0 false positives** (good emails wrongly binned).

Multiplying many word probabilities is exactly where the log trick from the softmax lesson helps: add log-probabilities instead, and nothing underflows.

## Common misconceptions

- **"P(A | B) is the same as P(B | A)."** Spotted given Dalmatian is near 1; Dalmatian given spotted is small.
- **"A 90% accurate test means a positive result is 90% likely to be right."** It depends on the **base rate**; for a rare condition it can be under 10%.
- **"New evidence should replace what you believed."** It should **update** it. The prior still counts.
- **"Bayesian probability is a different kind of maths."** It uses the **same rules**; only the interpretation (degree of belief) differs.
- **"Naive Bayes is useless because its assumption is wrong."** It's often a decent classifier anyway, though scikit-learn warns its probability outputs are "not to be taken too seriously".

## What you learned

- A **probability** is a number from 0 to 1, read either as a frequency or a **degree of belief**, with the same rules.
- **Conditional probability** P(A | B) zooms in on the cases where B is true, and **P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A)**.
- **Bayes' rule**: posterior = prior × likelihood ÷ P(evidence). Counting a crowd gives the same answer.
- **Base rates** matter: a positive result for a rare condition can still be unlikely to be real.
- In **odds form**, each piece of evidence multiplies the odds by its likelihood ratio.
- **Naive Bayes** combines words as independent evidence, and still filters spam well.
