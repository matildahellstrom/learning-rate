# The speedometer puzzle 🚗

Your car's speedometer reads 50 km/h. But what does that mean, **at this exact instant**?

Speed is distance divided by time. In a single instant, no time passes and the car moves no distance: zero divided by zero. 3Blue1Brown calls "instantaneous rate of change" an **oxymoron** for exactly this reason. And yet the needle points at 50, and it means something.

The idea that rescues it is the **derivative**, and it's the engine of machine learning: it tells a model which way to adjust each of its numbers to get a little less wrong.

This lesson covers what a derivative is, how to measure and calculate one, and the **chain rule**, which links derivatives through a long chain of steps like the layers of a neural network.

---

## Slope: rise over run

Start with a straight line. Its **slope** is how much it goes up for each step to the right: rise over run.

On the line y = 3x + 1, each step of 1 to the right goes up by 3. The slope is **3** everywhere.

A curve is harder: its steepness changes as you move along it. But zoom in far enough on any smooth curve and the piece under the microscope looks almost exactly like a straight line.

The **derivative** at a point is the slope of that zoomed-in line. It's written f′(x), or dy/dx: "the tiny change in y per tiny change in x". 3Blue1Brown suggests thinking of it not as change "in an instant" but as the **best constant rate of change** near that point.

---

## Measuring a derivative: nudge it

You can measure a derivative directly: **nudge** the input by a tiny amount, and see how much the output moves.

Take f(x) = x² at x = 3. So f(3) = 9.

🤔 Predict: nudge x up to 3.001. Roughly how much does f change, per unit of nudge?

f(3.001) = 9.006001. The output rose by 0.006001 for a nudge of 0.001, which is **about 6** per unit. Smaller nudges get closer and closer to exactly 6.

So the derivative of x² at x = 3 is **6**.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: the limit definition">
The derivative is what the nudge ratio approaches as the nudge $h$ shrinks to zero:

$$
f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}
$$

For $f(x) = x^2$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{(x + h)^2 - x^2}{h} &= \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} \\
&= 2x + h
\end{aligned}
$$

As $h \to 0$ this becomes $2x$. At $x = 3$: 6, matching the nudge experiment (which gave $6 + 0.001$).

</Deeper>

---

## Shortcuts: the rules

Nudging works, but for common functions there are rules that give the exact answer.

- **Power rule:** the derivative of xⁿ is n·xⁿ⁻¹. So x² gives 2x, and x³ gives 3x².
- **Constants:** a number on its own (like + 7) has derivative 0, because it doesn't change.
- **Scaling and adding:** the derivative of 5x² is 5 × 2x = 10x, and the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives.

Check against the nudge: the derivative of x² is 2x, and at x = 3 that's **6**. ✓

And the straight line y = 3x + 1? Its derivative is 3 + 0 = **3**, the slope we already knew.

---

## What the sign tells you

The derivative's **sign** says which way the curve is heading:

- **Positive:** going uphill. Increase x and the output rises.
- **Negative:** going downhill. Increase x and the output falls.
- **Zero:** flat. You're at the bottom of a valley, the top of a hill, or on a level stretch.

Its **size** says how steep: a derivative of −3 means each small nudge up in x lowers the output about 3 times as much.

This is exactly what learning needs. The gradient descent lesson treats a model's error (its **loss**) as a landscape: the derivative says which way is uphill, so the model steps the other way.

---

## Chains of functions

Real calculations are rarely one step. Usually one quantity feeds the next, which feeds the next: x goes into u, and u goes into y.

Think of three gears. Gear U turns **3 times** as fast as gear X, and gear Y turns **2 times** as fast as gear U.

🤔 Predict: how fast does Y turn compared with X?

**6 times** as fast: 3 × 2. You multiply, not add.

That's the **chain rule**: when steps are chained, their rates **multiply**. 3Blue1Brown's chain rule lesson pictures it as a chain of nudges: a tiny nudge to x causes a nudge to u, which in turn causes a nudge to y.

**dy/dx = dy/du × du/dx**

The notation makes it easy to remember: the du's look as if they cancel. (They aren't really fractions, but the rule behaves as if they were.)

---

## The chain rule at work

Let's find the derivative of y = (3x + 1)² at x = 1.

Split it into a chain:

- Inner step: u = 3x + 1, so du/dx = **3**.
- Outer step: y = u², so dy/du = **2u**.

At x = 1, u = 4, so dy/du = 8. Multiply: dy/dx = 8 × 3 = **24**.

Check it by nudging. At x = 1, y = 4² = 16. At x = 1.001, u = 4.003 and y = 16.024009. The rise is 0.024009 for a nudge of 0.001, so **about 24**. ✓

<Deeper title="Go deeper: longer chains">
For three steps, $y = f(g(h(x)))$, the rule just keeps multiplying:

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = f'(g(h(x))) \cdot g'(h(x)) \cdot h'(x)
$$

Each factor is a **local** derivative: how one step's output responds to its own input, measured at the value that actually flowed through it. That's why a long chain is easy to handle piece by piece. With several inputs the same rule applies to each one separately, using partial derivatives (the gradients lesson).

</Deeper>

---

## Why AI runs on the chain rule

A neural network is a long chain: input → layer 1 → layer 2 → … → output → **loss** (one number for how wrong it was). To learn, it needs the derivative of the loss with respect to **every** weight, and large models have an enormous number of them.

Nudging each weight one at a time would mean running the network once per weight. Instead, networks use **backpropagation**. The Deep Learning book describes it as an algorithm that computes the chain rule "with a specific order of operations that is highly efficient": start at the loss and work **backwards**, multiplying local derivatives layer by layer and reusing each result for everything earlier in the chain.

The CS231n course notes put it as computing gradients "through recursive application of chain rule". So every time a model learns from an example, the chain rule from this lesson is doing the work.

---

## Common misconceptions

- **"A derivative is a change in an instant."** Nothing changes in an instant. The derivative is what the rate of change approaches over smaller and smaller nudges.
- **"A derivative of zero means the function is zero."** It means the function is **flat** there. f(x) = x² + 5 has derivative 0 at x = 0, where its value is 5.
- **"In a chain, you add the rates."** You **multiply** them, like gears.
- **"dy/dx is a fraction you can cancel freely."** It behaves like one in the chain rule, which makes it a handy memory aid, but it's really a limit.
- **"Backpropagation is a special kind of maths."** It's the ordinary chain rule, organised so the work is shared.

## What you learned

- The **derivative** is the slope of a curve at one point: what "rise over run" approaches for tiny **nudges**.
- Rules like the **power rule** (xⁿ → n·xⁿ⁻¹) give exact answers; nudging is a handy check.
- Its **sign** says uphill or downhill; **zero** means flat, as at the bottom of a valley.
- The **chain rule** multiplies rates through a chain: dy/dx = dy/du × du/dx.
- **Backpropagation** applies the chain rule backwards through a network to get the derivative for every weight.
