# Which song comes next? 🎵

Imagine a music app that scores every song on three qualities from 0 to 10: energy, acoustic sound and danceability. (A made-up, simplified example; real apps learn much richer descriptions.)

You've just played a song scored **[8, 1, 9]**: loud and danceable. Two candidates are left:

- Song B: **[7, 2, 8]**
- Song C: **[2, 9, 1]**

You can probably tell that B is the better match. But how would a computer **put a number on** "alike"?

This lesson covers the **dot product**, the one-line calculation that answers that question, and **cosine similarity**, its fairer cousin. Both run inside search engines, recommenders, language models and image models.

---

## Multiply matching entries, then add

The **dot product** of two vectors of the same size: multiply the entries in matching positions, then add everything up. The result is a **single number**.

[2, 1] · [3, 4] = 2 × 3 + 1 × 4 = **10**

For the songs:

- Your song · B = 8 × 7 + 1 × 2 + 9 × 8 = 56 + 2 + 72 = **130**
- Your song · C = 8 × 2 + 1 × 9 + 9 × 1 = 16 + 9 + 9 = **34**

B wins by a mile. Each product is big only when **both** songs score high on the same quality, so the dot product rewards agreement.

You've seen this before: in the vectors and matrices lesson, each row of a matrix was combined with a vector this way. **A matrix times a vector is just a stack of dot products.**

---

## What the number means

The dot product has a neat geometric meaning. If you draw both vectors as arrows,

**a · b = length of a × length of b × cos(angle between them)**

The cosine of the angle is 1 when the arrows point the same way, 0 at a right angle, and −1 when they point in opposite directions. So:

- **Large and positive:** pointing roughly the same way.
- **Zero:** at right angles. The vectors are called **orthogonal**.
- **Negative:** pointing roughly opposite ways.

3Blue1Brown offers a picture for this: shadow one arrow onto the line of the other (a **projection**), then multiply the shadow's length by the other arrow's length, counting it as negative if the shadow points backwards. Arrows at right angles cast no shadow at all.

🤔 Predict: what is [1, 0] · [0, 1]?

1 × 0 + 0 × 1 = **0**. One arrow points right, the other points up: a right angle, so nothing in common.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: why the cosine appears">
The two formulas agree because of the law of cosines. For arrows $a$ and $b$ with angle $\theta$ between them, the third side of the triangle is $a - b$, and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|a - b\|^2 &= \|a\|^2 + \|b\|^2 \\
&\quad - 2\|a\|\|b\|\cos\theta
\end{aligned}
$$

Expanding the left side entry by entry gives $\|a\|^2 + \|b\|^2 - 2\,a \cdot b$. Matching the two lines leaves

$$
a \cdot b = \|a\|\|b\|\cos\theta
$$

which is how the Deep Learning book states it (equation 2.34). The dot product is also symmetric: $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$.

</Deeper>

---

## The trouble with length

The dot product mixes up two things: **direction** (do the vectors agree?) and **length** (how big are they?).

🤔 Predict: Song D is scored **[14, 4, 16]**, exactly double Song B, as if every score had been inflated. Is it a better match for your song than B?

Its dot product with your song is **260**, twice B's 130. But it has the same mix of qualities as B; it's just "louder" in every number. Whether the dot product should reward that depends on the task. For "is this the same **kind** of song?", it shouldn't.

The same problem shows up with text. If a document is a vector of word counts, a long essay has bigger numbers than a short note, so its raw dot product is bigger with **everything**.

---

## Cosine similarity: direction only

The fix is to divide out the lengths, leaving just the cosine of the angle:

**cosine similarity = (a · b) ÷ (length of a × length of b)**

The scikit-learn docs describe it as the dot product of the vectors after each has been scaled to length 1. The answer always lands between **−1** (opposite) and **1** (same direction), whatever the vectors' sizes.

A worked example with [3, 4] and [4, 3]:

- Dot product: 3 × 4 + 4 × 3 = 24
- Lengths: both are 5 (Pythagoras again)
- Cosine similarity: 24 ÷ 25 = **0.96**, very similar

And song D now scores **exactly the same** as song B against your song, because D points in the same direction as B. Doubling a vector doesn't change its direction.

---

## Meaning as direction

Here's where it gets exciting. Models can learn vectors for **words** (the embeddings lesson shows how), and the directions turn out to carry meaning.

In 2013, Mikolov, Yih and Zweig looked at the word vectors learned by a neural network trained to predict text. They found that relationships show up as consistent **offsets**. Their headline example: the vector for "King" − "Man" + "Woman" results in a vector **very close to** "Queen".

"Close" here is measured by direction, with cosine similarity. A common recipe for semantic search uses the same idea: turn the question and every document into vectors, then return the documents whose vectors point most nearly the same way as the question's.

---

## Dot products everywhere

Once you know the dot product, you start to see it all over AI:

- **Neural network layers** multiply a matrix by a vector: one dot product per output.
- **Convolutions** in image models slide a small grid over a picture and take a dot product at each spot (the convolutions lesson).
- **Attention** in a Transformer scores how relevant each word is to another with a dot product between two vectors (the attention lesson).
- **CLIP** matches photos with captions using cosine similarity (the CLIP lesson).

One detail from the original Transformer paper: the attention dot products are divided by √(d_k), where d_k is the size of the vectors. The authors suspected that with big vectors the dot products grow large, which pushes the next step (a softmax) into a region where learning slows to a crawl.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: why big vectors give big dot products">
The paper's own illustration: suppose every entry of $q$ and $k$ is an independent random number with mean 0 and variance 1. Then

$$
q \cdot k = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i
$$

has mean 0 and variance $d_k$: each of the $d_k$ products adds its own variance of 1. The typical size (standard deviation) grows like $\sqrt{d_k}$, so dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ brings it back to about 1.

This is **not** the same as cosine similarity: the lengths of the actual vectors are not divided out, only a fixed number based on their size.

</Deeper>

## Common misconceptions

- **"The dot product gives a vector."** It gives a single number. Adding vectors gives a vector.
- **"A dot product of 0 means one vector is zero."** Not necessarily: [1, 0] · [0, 1] = 0 because the vectors are at right angles.
- **"A bigger dot product always means more similar."** Only if the lengths are comparable. Longer vectors give bigger dot products; cosine similarity removes that effect.
- **"Cosine similarity runs from 0 to 1."** It runs from −1 to 1. Some kinds of data (like word counts, which are never negative) only produce 0 to 1, but in general negative values mean opposite directions.
- **"Similar vectors must have similar entries."** For cosine similarity, [1, 2] and [100, 200] are identical (similarity 1), even though their entries are very different.

## What you learned

- The **dot product** multiplies matching entries and adds them up, giving one number.
- It equals **length × length × cos(angle)**: positive when vectors agree, **zero** at right angles, negative when they oppose.
- A matrix times a vector is a **stack of dot products**.
- Raw dot products grow with **length**; **cosine similarity** divides the lengths out and runs from −1 to 1.
- Learned vectors can carry **meaning in their direction**, which is why similarity search works.
