# Describe a strawberry to a computer 🍓

You know a strawberry when you see one. A computer doesn't see anything: it only stores numbers.

So how could you describe a strawberry to it? One idea: pick a few qualities and score each one. Sweetness 8 out of 10, redness 9, size 2. Now the strawberry is **[8, 9, 2]**. A lemon might be **[2, 1, 4]**.

That little list is a **vector**, and grids of them are **matrices**. Every photo, word and sound a model handles becomes one of these, and every layer of a neural network is built from one operation on them.

This lesson covers what vectors and matrices are, how to add and scale them, and how to multiply a matrix by a vector.

---

## A vector is an ordered list

A **vector** is a list of numbers in a fixed order. Each position means something: in the fruit example, position 1 is sweetness, 2 is redness and 3 is size. The number of entries is the vector's **dimension**, so [8, 9, 2] is 3-dimensional.

You meet vectors all the time without the name:

- A colour on a screen is [red, green, blue], for example [255, 0, 0] for pure red.
- A spot on a map is [east, north].
- A day's weather could be [temperature, rain, wind].

🤔 Predict: is the vector [2, 5] the same as [5, 2]?

No. Order is the whole point. On a map, 2 steps east and 5 north is a different place from 5 east and 2 north. A vector is not a bag of numbers; each slot has a job.

---

## Vectors as arrows

A 2-D vector can also be drawn as an **arrow** from the origin (0, 0). The vector [3, 4] is the arrow that goes 3 right and 4 up.

3Blue1Brown's _Essence of linear algebra_ series makes the point that it doesn't matter which view you call the real one: lists and arrows are two descriptions of the same thing, and it pays to switch between them.

That picture gives us a new idea: **length**. The arrow [3, 4] makes a right-angled triangle with sides 3 and 4, so by Pythagoras its length is

√(3² + 4²) = √25 = **5**.

The length of a vector is also called its **norm**, written ‖v‖. The same recipe works in any dimension: square every entry, add them up, take the square root.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: the Euclidean norm">
For a vector $x$ with $n$ entries, the usual length is the **L2 norm**

$$
\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \dots + x_n^2}
$$

The Deep Learning book calls it the **Euclidean norm**: the ordinary straight-line distance from the origin to the point $x$. It is used so often in machine learning that the subscript 2 is usually dropped.

Other norms exist. The L1 norm adds up the absolute values instead, $|x_1| + \dots + |x_n|$, so [3, 4] has L1 norm 7.

</Deeper>

---

## Adding and scaling

Two operations do almost all the work.

**Adding** two vectors of the same dimension: add matching entries.

[1, 2] + [3, 1] = [4, 3]

As arrows, this is "walk along the first, then along the second": you end up at the tip of [4, 3].

**Scaling** a vector by a number: multiply every entry by it.

3 × [1, 2] = [3, 6]

As arrows, scaling by 3 makes the arrow 3 times longer in the same direction. Scaling by −1 flips it round to point the opposite way.

🤔 Predict: what is the length of 2 × [3, 4]?

The vector is [6, 8], and √(36 + 64) = **10**. Scaling by 2 doubles the length, as you'd hope.

---

## Real models use huge vectors

Three numbers can't capture much about a strawberry. Real models use far longer vectors.

- The original Transformer (Vaswani and colleagues, 2017), the design behind today's chatbots, represents each word piece as a vector of **512** numbers.
- GPT-3 (Brown and colleagues, 2020) uses vectors of **12,288** numbers in its largest version.

Nobody can picture an arrow in 12,288 dimensions. The good news is that you don't have to. Adding, scaling and length work exactly as they do in 2-D: you just do the same thing to more entries.

In these models the individual numbers aren't hand-picked qualities like "sweetness". They are **learned** during training, and the embeddings lesson shows how.

## A matrix is a grid

A **matrix** is a rectangular grid of numbers. Its **shape** is written rows × columns, so this one is 2 × 3:

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}
$$

You can read a matrix as a stack of vectors. A table of 100 fruits, each scored on 3 qualities, is a 100 × 3 matrix: one row per fruit.

A greyscale photo is a matrix too: one number per pixel for its brightness. (The images-as-tensors lesson takes this further, to colour photos and batches of them.)

---

## Multiplying a matrix by a vector

Here is the operation at the heart of every neural network. To multiply a matrix by a vector, take **each row** of the matrix, multiply its entries by the matching entries of the vector, and **add them up**. Each row gives one number.

With the matrix A above and the vector [2, 1, 4]:

- Row 1: 1 × 2 + 0 × 1 + 2 × 4 = **10**
- Row 2: 3 × 2 + 1 × 1 + 0 × 4 = **7**

So A times [2, 1, 4] is **[10, 7]**.

🤔 Predict: A has 3 columns. What would happen if you tried to multiply it by a vector with only 2 entries?

It doesn't work: every row has 3 numbers, and there would be nothing to pair the third one with. **The vector's length must match the number of columns.** The output has one entry per row.

This is exactly what a layer in a neural network does. The matrix holds the layer's learned **weights**: 3 numbers go in, 2 come out, and each output is a weighted mix of all the inputs.

---

## Multiplying two matrices

You can also multiply a matrix by another matrix: treat each **column** of the second matrix as a vector and multiply it as before. The results become the columns of the answer.

That gives a simple **shape rule**. An m × n matrix times an n × p matrix gives an m × p matrix. The inner sizes must match, and the outer sizes survive:

(2 × **3**) times (**3** × 4) gives 2 × 4.

Two surprises for anyone used to ordinary numbers:

- **Order matters.** AB and BA are usually different, and often one of them doesn't even exist. In the example above, a 3 × 4 times a 2 × 3 is not allowed.
- Multiplying matrices means **doing one step after another**. 3Blue1Brown's series shows each matrix as a way of moving space around, and the product as doing both moves in turn.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: the formula">
If $C = AB$, the entry in row $i$ and column $j$ of $C$ is

$$
C_{i,j} = \sum_k A_{i,k} B_{k,j}
$$

The sum runs over the inner size, which is why it has to match. Matrix multiplication is not commutative (in general $AB \ne BA$), but it is associative, $A(BC) = (AB)C$, and distributive, $A(B + C) = AB + AC$.

The **transpose** $A^\top$ flips a matrix over its diagonal, turning rows into columns. It reverses products: $(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top$.

</Deeper>

## Common misconceptions

- **"A vector is just a list, so order doesn't matter."** Each position has a meaning; swapping entries gives a different vector.
- **"You can add any two vectors."** Only vectors of the same dimension. [1, 2] + [1, 2, 3] is not defined.
- **"Matrix multiplication multiplies matching entries."** That's a different operation (the element-wise or Hadamard product). Matrix multiplication combines each row with each column.
- **"AB is the same as BA."** Usually not, and often only one of them exists.
- **"Vectors are only for arrows and physics."** In machine learning a vector is any list of numbers describing something: a pixel, a word, a user.

## What you learned

- A **vector** is an ordered list of numbers; its **dimension** is how many there are.
- Vectors can be drawn as **arrows**; their **length (norm)** comes from Pythagoras.
- You **add** vectors entry by entry and **scale** them by multiplying every entry.
- A **matrix** is a grid with a shape of rows × columns.
- A **matrix times a vector** combines each row with the vector, giving one number per row. This is what a neural network layer does.
- Matrix products follow the **shape rule** (inner sizes match, outer sizes survive), and **order matters**.
