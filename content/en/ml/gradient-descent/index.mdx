# Lost in the fog ⛰️

Imagine you're on a mountainside in thick fog. You want to get down to the valley, but you can only see the ground right under your feet.

A sensible plan: **feel which way the ground slopes, take a small step downhill, repeat.**

That's it. That's **gradient descent**, the algorithm that trains almost every neural network, from spam filters to large language models.

---

## Why would a computer want to go downhill?

Training a model means tuning its numbers, its **parameters**, until it makes good predictions.

We measure how bad the predictions are with a **loss**: one number, where lower is better. Picture the loss as the height of a landscape, and every possible setting of the parameters as a spot on it.

Learning means finding a low spot. Gradient descent is how we walk there.

## The update rule

Every step follows one rule:

**new position = old position − learning rate × slope**

- The **slope** (the derivative) says which way is uphill, so we go the opposite way. That's the minus sign.
- The **learning rate**, written η ("eta"), says how bold each step is.

<Deeper>
For a function $f$, gradient descent iterates

$$
x_{k+1} = x_k - \eta \, f'(x_k)
$$

On the bowl $f(x) = x^2$ we have $f'(x) = 2x$, so

$$
x_{k+1} = x_k - 2\eta x_k = (1 - 2\eta)\, x_k
$$

Every step multiplies the distance to the minimum by the same factor $r = 1 - 2\eta$, so $x_k = r^k x_0$: a geometric sequence. It shrinks to zero exactly when $|r| < 1$, that is, for $0 < \eta < 1$.

- $0 < \eta < \tfrac12$: $r$ is positive and the ball creeps in from one side.
- $\tfrac12 < \eta < 1$: $r$ is negative, so the ball overshoots and zig-zags, but still converges.
- $\eta > 1$: $|r| > 1$ and every step overshoots more than the last. Divergence!

At $\eta = \tfrac12$ we get $r = 0$: the ball lands on the minimum **in a single step**. For any parabola the perfect step is $\eta = 1 / f''$, which is Newton's method in disguise.

</Deeper>

## Too timid, too bold

The learning rate is the most important knob in machine learning. This one starts out bold:

<GradientPlayground landscape="bowl" learningRate={0.95} />

Try these and watch what happens:

- **0.02**: painfully slow. You'll be here all day.
- **0.95**: the ball flies across the valley, again and again.
- **1.05**: each jump is bigger than the last, and the loss explodes. 💥

Real training runs fail in exactly these ways, which is why people spend so much time tuning the learning rate.

## Stuck in the wrong valley

Real loss landscapes aren't perfect bowls. This one has **two valleys**, and the left one is deeper.

<GradientPlayground
  landscape="doubleWell"
  choices={['doubleWell', 'bumpy']}
  start={2}
  learningRate={0.05}
/>

Start on the right and press **Play**. The ball settles in the shallow valley and stays there. It only ever feels the slope under its feet, so it has no idea a deeper valley exists.

That's called a **local minimum**. Try dragging the ball to the left side, or switch to the bumpy landscape.

<Deeper title="Go deeper: is this a problem in practice?">
In one dimension local minima are everywhere. Neural networks have millions or billions of parameters, and there the picture changes. For a point to be a minimum, the loss must curve upwards in **every** direction at once, and with millions of directions that's rare. Most flat spots are **saddle points**: uphill in some directions, downhill in others. Descent eventually slides off them.

Real training also uses **stochastic** gradient descent: each step estimates the slope from a small random batch of data, so the steps are noisy. That noise helps shake the ball out of narrow, shallow dips.

</Deeper>

## Two dimensions: the ravine

Real models have many parameters, so the landscape has many directions. Here are two, seen from above. The lines are **contours**, like on a hiking map, and the green dot is the bottom.

With more than one direction, the slope becomes the **gradient**: an arrow made of one slope per direction, pointing straight uphill. We step against it.

<RavinePlayground />

This valley is **10 times steeper across than along**. Turn up the learning rate and watch: the ball bounces between the steep walls while it barely crawls along the gentle floor.

<Deeper>
The ravine is $f(x, y) = \tfrac12 (x^2 + \kappa y^2)$ with $\kappa = 10$. Its gradient is

$$
\nabla f(x, y) = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ \kappa y \end{pmatrix}
$$

so the two coordinates evolve independently:

$$
x_{k+1} = (1 - \eta)\, x_k, \qquad y_{k+1} = (1 - \eta\kappa)\, y_k
$$

The steep direction is stable only if $|1 - \eta\kappa| < 1$, which means $\eta < 2/\kappa = 0.2$. But then the gentle direction shrinks by a factor of at least $1 - 0.2 = 0.8$ per step: slow.

The ratio $\kappa$ between the steepest and the gentlest curvature is called the **condition number**. Badly conditioned problems force a small learning rate and slow progress. That is exactly the problem **momentum** and **Adam** were invented to fix.

</Deeper>

## What you learned

- **Loss** measures how wrong a model is. Training means making it small.
- **Gradient descent** repeats one move: measure the slope, step the opposite way.
- The **learning rate** sets the step size. Too small is slow; too big overshoots or diverges.
- Descent can get stuck in **local minima**, and it struggles in **ravines** where some directions are much steeper than others.
